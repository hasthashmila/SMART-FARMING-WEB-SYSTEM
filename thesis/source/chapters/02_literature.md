# CHAPTER 2 — LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Chapter Overview

This chapter reviews the research and technologies relevant to the design of the Smart Farming Web System. Rather than treating each technology in isolation, the review is organised thematically around the four interrelated strands that together define the design space of the artefact: smart-agriculture architectures, IoT and sensor networks in precision farming, AI agents and autonomous decision-making (including explainability), and agricultural e-commerce and B2B platforms. A fifth, cross-cutting strand, digital twins, is treated within the architecture discussion because it is the integrating metaphor of the proposed interface. Following the guidance of the Faculty, the chapter opens with a conceptual map (Section 2.2) and a brief domain overview (Section 2.3), devotes approximately a third of its length to a comparative assessment of existing systems and frameworks (Section 2.4), and the majority of its length to a technological analysis covering algorithms, design and workflow (Section 2.5). Throughout, the researcher's own reflections are set against the reviewed literature, and every decision to adopt or reject an approach is justified. The chapter closes with a reflection that links the findings to the research gap (Section 2.6) and a summary (Section 2.7).

## 2.3 Domain Overview

Precision agriculture, sometimes termed smart farming or Agriculture 4.0, refers to the management of agricultural production using information technologies that observe, measure and respond to variability within and between fields (Wolfert et al., 2017; Abbasi, Martinez and Ahmad, 2022). Its origins lie in the site-specific crop management of the 1990s, which relied on satellite positioning and yield mapping on large mechanised farms. The proliferation of low-cost microcontrollers, wireless connectivity, cloud computing and machine learning in the 2010s extended the concept to smaller holdings and to continuous, sensor-based observation of the growing environment (Tzounis et al., 2017; Elijah et al., 2018).

Three trends characterise the current state of the domain. First, sensing has become cheap and pervasive: a microcontroller node capable of reporting soil moisture, temperature, humidity and nutrient status now costs less than a bag of fertiliser (Ayaz et al., 2019). Second, the volume and variety of data collected exceed what farmers can interpret unaided, which has shifted research attention from data acquisition to analytics and decision support (Kamilaris, Kartakoullis and Prenafeta-Boldú, 2017; Liakos et al., 2018). Third, the agri-food value chain is digitising downstream of the farm gate as well, with online marketplaces, traceability systems and digital trade documentation becoming preconditions for access to premium export markets (Lezoche et al., 2020; Zhang and Berghäll, 2021).

In Sri Lanka, these trends intersect with a farming population that is predominantly smallholder, linguistically diverse (Sinhala, Tamil and English) and unevenly served by digital infrastructure and digital literacy (Klerkx, Jakku and Labarthe, 2019). The researcher's reflection is that this context sharpens, rather than weakens, the case for integration: a smallholder cannot afford to purchase, learn and reconcile several separate systems, so a single platform that is usable in the farmer's own language and that takes the farmer from field observation to market outcome is more likely to be adopted than a collection of specialised point solutions.

## 2.4 Existing Systems, Frameworks and Designs

This section reviews existing smart-agriculture systems and frameworks reported in the literature and in commercial practice, assesses their suitability for the problem investigated in this study, and records the researcher's reflections on each.

### 2.4.1 Academic IoT Platforms for Precision Farming

Jayaraman et al. (2016) describe SmartFarmNet, an IoT platform developed in Australia that collects environmental, soil and fertilisation data through heterogeneous sensors, stores it in a cloud back end and provides analytics and visualisation to farmers. The platform demonstrated that a generic, extensible ingest layer with a common data model can accommodate many sensor types and that near-real-time visualisation is valued by farmers. Its limitations, acknowledged by the authors, are the absence of automated decision-making and the fact that recommendations remained the responsibility of a human agronomist. For the present research, SmartFarmNet is suitable as a reference for the ingest and storage layers (its normalised data model directly informed the alias-based field normalisation in Section 5.4) but unsuitable as a complete solution, because it does not close the loop from observation to action.

Kamienski et al. (2019) present the SWAMP platform for IoT-based precision irrigation, deployed in Brazil, Italy and Spain. SWAMP is architecturally notable for its explicit separation of data acquisition, data management, analytics and application layers, and for its use of FIWARE context brokers to represent field entities. It automates irrigation prescriptions using soil-water models. The platform is relevant because it demonstrates automated, verifiable actuation, which the present study adopts in the form of the workflow verification stage. It is unsuitable as a direct solution because its irrigation focus excludes nutrient management, its models require calibration data unavailable for Sri Lankan smallholder soils, and it has no commerce component.

Khanna and Kaur (2019) and Kour and Arora (2020) survey several dozen academic IoT-agriculture systems. Both surveys conclude that the majority of systems concentrate on monitoring and visualisation; fewer than a quarter implement any automated decision logic, and almost none extend beyond the farm to market functions. Sinha and Dhanalakshmi (2022) reach the same conclusion in a more recent survey and add that data-quality handling (validation, cleaning, handling of missing values) is rarely described in detail, despite being critical to the reliability of downstream analytics. The researcher's reflection is that these surveys justify two design decisions of the present study: treating the data-preparation pipeline as a first-class, documented component, and treating the decision and commerce layers as core rather than optional.

### 2.4.2 Commercial Farm-Management and Sensing Platforms

Commercial farm-management platforms such as Climate FieldView, John Deere Operations Center and Farmers Edge offer sophisticated field mapping, machine data integration and agronomic analytics. They are designed for large, mechanised farms in North America and Europe, are priced per hectare or per machine, and assume high-bandwidth connectivity and English-language users. Sensor-focused products such as CropX, Sentek and Arable provide soil-moisture and weather probes with proprietary cloud dashboards and irrigation recommendations. These products validate the market for sensor-driven decision support but are unsuitable for this research on three grounds: their cost and hardware lock-in are incompatible with smallholder economics; their decision logic is proprietary and opaque, which conflicts with the explainability requirement; and none integrates a B2B marketplace. Their user-interface conventions (zone-based dashboards, colour-coded status, trend charts) were nevertheless studied and adapted for the digital-twin interface described in Chapter 5.

### 2.4.3 Agricultural E-Commerce and Marketplace Platforms

Agricultural e-commerce platforms fall into three groups (Zhang and Berghäll, 2021). Business-to-consumer platforms such as Pinduoduo's agricultural channel in China connect farmers directly to consumers and have achieved enormous scale, but their consumer focus and reliance on integrated logistics networks make them a poor model for export-oriented B2B trade. B2B trade platforms such as Tridge and Alibaba's agricultural category aggregate supplier listings and buyer requests for quotation internationally; they provide structured product information, messaging and, in some cases, verification services, but they are entirely disconnected from farm operations, so listings reflect the supplier's claims rather than verifiable production data. Regional initiatives such as Sri Lanka's Govi Mithuru advisory service and various government market-price portals offer information but not transaction workflows. The researcher's reflection is that the B2B trade platforms establish the functional vocabulary that buyers expect (grade, certification, quantity, destination, inquiry, quotation, order, documents) and that the present study should adopt this vocabulary while adding the missing linkage to live field data. This reflection is realised in the marketplace design in Section 5.8, where each listing may be linked to a monitored zone and carries its live status.

### 2.4.4 Digital-Twin Systems in Agriculture

Verdouw et al. (2021) provide the definitive typology of digital twins in smart farming, distinguishing imaginary, monitoring, predictive, prescriptive, autonomous and recollection twins according to the degree of coupling between the physical and virtual objects. They report that most agricultural twins in the literature are monitoring twins, and that prescriptive and autonomous twins, which recommend or execute actions, are rare. Pylianidis, Osinga and Athanasiadis (2021) similarly find that agricultural digital twins are at an early stage and often amount to dashboards with a twin label. Grieves and Vickers (2017) stress that the value of a twin lies in the bidirectional flow between the physical asset and its virtual representation. The researcher's reflection is that the present study's twin should be positioned deliberately as a prescriptive twin with an optional autonomous mode: the virtual zone is updated from field readings, the agent prescribes actions, and, when approved, actuator commands flow back to the field and are verified against subsequent readings. This positioning is more ambitious than the monitoring twins that dominate the literature and is justified by the study's aim of improving decision timeliness, not merely visibility.


### 2.4.6 Sri Lankan and Regional Initiatives

Several initiatives closer to the study context deserve mention. The Department of Agriculture's Govi Mithuru mobile advisory service, delivered in Sinhala and Tamil, provides calendar-based crop advice by SMS and voice and has demonstrated that farmers will adopt a digital service in their own language; it does not, however, sense field conditions or transact produce. Government price-information portals publish wholesale prices for major crops but offer no linkage to individual producers. In India, the e-NAM national agricultural market and several state-level sensor-advisory pilots have shown that market and monitoring functions each succeed separately at scale, while university projects in Bangladesh, Kenya and Vietnam reported in the surveys above have demonstrated low-cost ESP32 irrigation controllers. The researcher's reflection is that these initiatives collectively validate the individual components of the proposed platform in developing-country conditions, in particular the readiness of farmers to use Sinhala- and Tamil-language services, and that the contribution of the present research is to connect them within one architecture rather than to reinvent any one of them.

## 2.5 Technological Analysis

This section, which constitutes the greater part of the review, analyses the technologies from which the artefact is constructed under three headings: algorithmic analysis (sensing, validation and decision algorithms), design analysis (architectural and interface design) and workflow analysis (orchestration, automation and commerce processes).

### 2.5.1 Algorithmic Analysis

#### 2.5.1.1 Sensing technologies for the target parameters

The nine parameters targeted by this study are those identified by the literature as most directly relevant to crop health, irrigation scheduling and nutrient management (Ayaz et al., 2019; Tzounis et al., 2017). Soil moisture may be measured by resistive probes, which are cheap but corrode and drift, or by capacitive probes, which are more stable and are therefore preferred for continuous deployment (Elijah et al., 2018). Soil NPK is commonly measured with RS485 electrochemical sensors that return concentrations in mg/kg; their absolute accuracy is limited and they are best used to detect trends and threshold crossings rather than to prescribe exact fertiliser quantities, a limitation that shaped the conservative "recommend a dose after approval" rule adopted in Chapter 5. Temperature and humidity are reliably measured by digital sensors such as the DHT22 and DS18B20; pH probes require calibration and are sensitive to temperature; and tipping-bucket rain gauges provide event counts that must be converted to millimetres. The researcher's reflection is that sensor error is inevitable, and that the software must therefore be designed to detect and reject implausible readings rather than to assume clean data, an insight that led to the range-based validation rules of Section 5.4.

#### 2.5.1.2 Communication protocols

Naik (2017) compares MQTT, CoAP, AMQP and HTTP for IoT systems. MQTT, a lightweight publish–subscribe protocol, offers small message overhead, quality-of-service levels and resilience to intermittent connectivity, which makes it the dominant choice for battery-powered field nodes (Light, 2017). HTTP, by contrast, is universally supported, stateless and simple to secure with standard web mechanisms, and for a node that reports every few seconds over Wi-Fi the additional overhead is negligible. The researcher's decision, justified in Section 3.7 and Chapter 5, was to make HTTP with a per-device key the primary ingest interface, because it requires no broker and can be deployed anywhere a web server runs, while documenting an MQTT topic convention so that a Mosquitto bridge can forward broker messages to the same endpoint for deployments that need queuing and offline buffering. This hybrid approach follows the thesis's stated intention to support "MQTT and HTTP-based communication protocols".

#### 2.5.1.3 Data validation and preparation

Sinha and Dhanalakshmi (2022) and Kamilaris, Kartakoullis and Prenafeta-Boldú (2017) emphasise that raw agricultural sensor data are noisy, incomplete and heterogeneous in units and formats, and that analytics built on unvalidated data produce unreliable results. The standard preparation steps are: normalisation of field names and units; detection of missing values; range or plausibility checks; timestamp standardisation; and association of each measurement with a device and location identifier. Statistical outlier detection (for example z-score or interquartile filters) is sometimes added, but is fragile at low sampling rates and can suppress genuine rapid changes such as rain events. The researcher elected to implement deterministic, physically motivated range checks (for example, soil moisture must lie between 0 and 100 per cent and pH between 0 and 14) combined with a completeness score, and to expose rejected readings to the user so that sensor faults become visible rather than silently discarded. This choice is defended in Section 5.4.

#### 2.5.1.4 Decision algorithms: rule-based reasoning versus machine learning

Liakos et al. (2018) review machine-learning applications in agriculture and find yield prediction, disease detection and water management to be the most common. Such models require large labelled datasets and are difficult for farmers to interpret. Rule-based decision support, by contrast, encodes agronomic guidance directly as threshold rules on measured parameters and is transparent, auditable and easy to adapt to local crop policies (Russell and Norvig, 2021). Adadi and Berrada (2018), reviewing explainable artificial intelligence, note that in high-stakes, low-trust domains, intrinsically interpretable models are frequently preferable to post-hoc explanations of opaque models. Given the absence of labelled training data for Sri Lankan smallholder crops, the study's explicit explainability requirement and the need for local agronomists to inspect and adjust the decision policy, the researcher adopted a rule-based decision-intelligence approach in which each rule records the readings, thresholds and confidence that produced its output. Machine learning is retained as a future extension (Chapter 7) once the platform has accumulated sufficient labelled data. This decision was validated by the evaluation results in Chapter 6, where the rule engine achieved the target agreement with expert-defined scenarios.

#### 2.5.1.5 AI agents and decision intelligence

Wooldridge and Jennings (1995) define an intelligent agent as a software entity that perceives its environment, reasons about it and acts on it autonomously to achieve goals, and distinguish reactive agents, which map perceptions to actions through condition–action rules, from deliberative agents, which maintain explicit models and plans. Russell and Norvig (2021) extend this taxonomy to model-based and goal-based agents. Decision intelligence, as described in the industry literature, provides a conceptual basis for linking raw observations, defined rules, predictive models and resulting actions into a coherent decision pipeline. In the present study the AI agent is a model-based reactive agent: it maintains a model of each zone (its crop policy, latest validated reading, recent history and node status), applies a prioritised rule set and emits decisions with evidence. This is deliberately simpler than a planning agent, and the researcher's reflection is that the simplicity is a strength in a safety-relevant domain, because every action the agent proposes can be traced to a named rule and a set of measured values.

#### 2.5.1.6 Natural-language interaction

Natural-language interfaces lower the barrier to use for people with limited digital literacy. Full conversational agents based on large language models offer fluent interaction but require internet access to third-party services, raise data-privacy concerns and are difficult to constrain to reliable, evidence-based answers. A lightweight intent classifier based on multilingual keyword matching, by contrast, runs locally, is fully deterministic and can compose answers from the same validated data and decisions that drive the dashboard. The researcher adopted the latter for the trilingual farm assistant, accepting a narrower range of understood questions in exchange for reliability and offline operation.

### 2.5.2 Design Analysis

#### 2.5.2.1 Layered smart-agriculture architectures

Smart-agriculture architectures in the literature commonly follow layered structural patterns in which physical sensors and actuators constitute a perception layer, communication technologies provide connectivity between field devices and backend infrastructure, data-processing services transform collected observations into usable information, application services present dashboards and recommendations to end users, and, in more advanced designs, business-oriented services support higher-level decision-making and commercial activity (Tzounis et al., 2017; Elijah et al., 2018). This layered architectural style provides a useful conceptual basis for the present research because it cleanly separates field devices, data processing, application logic and business operations, allowing each layer to evolve independently while preserving well-defined interfaces between them.

The system proposed in this research follows the same general layering principle while deliberately extending the conventional architecture with a dedicated AI-agent service layer and an integrated B2B commerce layer, giving the six-layer architecture presented in Chapter 4. This extension allows environmental observations captured at the field level to travel, in a traceable and auditable manner, through network and backend services into intelligent application functions and, where commercially relevant, into export-oriented workflows, a chain of value that is frequently interrupted in existing point solutions.

#### 2.5.2.2 Web application architecture and API design

Fielding (2000) defines the REST architectural style, whose stateless, resource-oriented interfaces have become the standard for web APIs. Sommerville (2016) recommends that web applications separate a thin, stateless HTTP layer from domain logic to support testability. For real-time updates, WebSockets and Server-Sent Events are the principal options; SSE is unidirectional, uses plain HTTP, reconnects automatically and is sufficient when the client only needs to be told that new data are available. The researcher chose REST for all read and write operations and SSE for push notifications, avoiding the additional complexity of WebSockets.

A further design consideration is dependency footprint. Many web platforms depend on hundreds of third-party packages, which complicates deployment in low-resource settings and introduces supply-chain risk (Ferrag et al., 2020). The researcher adopted a zero-dependency policy for the backend, using only Node.js built-in modules, so that the system can be started with a single command on any Node.js host; this decision is justified in Chapter 5.

#### 2.5.2.3 Interface design for low digital literacy and multilingual users

Nielsen (1994) established the usability heuristics (visibility of system status, match between system and real world, error prevention, recognition rather than recall) that remain the basis of interface evaluation, and Brooke (1996) provided the System Usability Scale used to measure perceived usability. Klerkx, Jakku and Labarthe (2019) and Rose and Chilvers (2018) argue that digital agriculture tools fail farmers when they assume English literacy and desktop use. The design implications adopted in this study are: a mobile-first responsive layout; colour-coded status that is reinforced by text and icons; recommendations phrased as actions with their reasons; and a complete interface in English, Sinhala and Tamil selected at login, with typography chosen for legibility of Sinhala and Tamil scripts. The researcher's reflection is that language is not a cosmetic feature but a determinant of adoption, and the evaluation in Chapter 6 accordingly recruited participants who used each language.

#### 2.5.2.4 Security and privacy design

Ferrag et al. (2020) review security and privacy in green IoT-based agriculture and identify device authentication, data integrity and access control as the principal requirements. Standard web security practice (Sommerville, 2016) requires salted, slow password hashing, session tokens with expiry, rate limiting of authentication attempts, HTTP-only cookies, input validation and security headers such as a Content Security Policy. Each of these was adopted in the artefact (Section 5.3), and per-device keys authenticate sensor nodes so that a compromised or misconfigured node can be isolated by rotating its key.


#### 2.5.2.5 Digital-twin design pattern

Verdouw et al. (2021) propose that an agricultural digital twin comprises a physical object (the field, crop or animal), a virtual object that mirrors its state, a data connection from physical to virtual (sensing) and, for prescriptive and autonomous twins, a connection from virtual to physical (actuation), together with services that operate on the virtual object. Translating this pattern into software terms yields a small set of design rules that the present study adopted. First, the virtual object must be a first-class entity with its own identity and state, not merely a view over raw readings; in the artefact each zone holds its crop policy, its latest validated reading, its recent history, its node status and its current decision set, and the dashboard renders that entity. Second, the sensing connection must include validation, because a twin that mirrors sensor faults is worse than no twin; hence the pipeline of Section 2.5.1.3. Third, the actuation connection must be verified against subsequent sensing, closing the loop, which is the purpose of the workflow verification stage. Fourth, the twin must be observable over time, which the analytics view and the decision log provide. Pylianidis, Osinga and Athanasiadis (2021) additionally warn that agricultural twins must remain interpretable to non-specialist users; the researcher's response was to make the twin's state and its reasoning visible in the user's language, rather than to pursue three-dimensional or geospatial fidelity that would add cost without adding decision value for a smallholder.

### 2.5.3 Workflow Analysis

#### 2.5.3.1 Workflow orchestration and traceability

Workflow orchestration defines how a decision can automatically trigger one or more subsequent system processes, for example generating an irrigation alert, updating a digital-twin visualisation or flagging a batch of produce for quality review. The literature on decision support in agriculture (Kamienski et al., 2019) and on autonomous systems generally (Russell and Norvig, 2021) stresses that automation must be bounded by human oversight and must verify its own effects. The present study models each workflow as an explicit state machine with detected, approval, execute, verify and completed stages, records who or what performed each transition, and treats failure to verify as a first-class outcome. Autonomous mode, which allows the agent to approve its own proposals, is an explicit, user-controlled setting rather than the default, following the principle that the farmer retains discretion over whether to act on a recommendation.

#### 2.5.3.2 Agri-food B2B and export process workflows

Lezoche et al. (2020) survey agri-food supply-chain technologies and describe the export process as a sequence of buyer–supplier negotiation, order confirmation, quality verification, documentation (commercial invoice, packing list, phytosanitary certificate, certificate of origin), payment and dispatch, with traceability required at each stage. Zhang and Berghäll (2021) find that e-commerce platforms in the agri-food sector add value primarily by structuring this interaction and reducing intermediation. The researcher adopted this sequence directly as the seven-stage export process of the B2B module (inquiry, quotation, buyer confirmation, quality verification, documents, dispatch, delivery), with role-based rules governing which party may advance each stage and with payment recorded through an explicit integration interface. The workflow is deliberately generic so that it can be connected to external logistics or payment services in future.

#### 2.5.3.3 Research and development workflow: DSRM and agile delivery

Hevner et al. (2004) established Design Science Research as a paradigm for information-systems research whose output is a purposeful artefact evaluated against its intended utility, and Peffers et al. (2007) formalised the six-activity DSRM process (problem identification, objectives, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, communication). Agile methods, in particular Scrum (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020), provide an iterative delivery workflow that fits naturally within DSRM's build-and-evaluate loop. The researcher combined the two, using DSRM as the research framework and Scrum sprints as the mechanism for the design-and-development activity, as described in Chapter 3.


#### 2.5.3.4 Evaluation approaches in smart-agriculture research

The reviewed literature evaluates smart-agriculture systems in four ways, and the choice among them shaped the evaluation plan of this study. Technical benchmarking measures packet delivery, latency, energy consumption and data completeness (Jayaraman et al., 2016; Kamienski et al., 2019); it is objective and reproducible but says nothing about decision quality or adoption. Agronomic trials measure water savings, yield or input cost over a season (Kamienski et al., 2019); they are the strongest evidence of value but require a full growing season and control plots, which exceed the time available to an undergraduate project. Decision-quality assessment compares system recommendations with expert judgement or with outcomes on labelled data (Liakos et al., 2018); for rule-based systems, scenario-based comparison with predefined expected outcomes is the practical equivalent. User-centred evaluation measures usability and perceived usefulness with instruments such as SUS and the Technology Acceptance Model (Brooke, 1996; Klerkx, Jakku and Labarthe, 2019). Hevner et al. (2004) require that a design-science artefact be evaluated rigorously with methods appropriate to its purpose, and Peffers et al. (2007) note that evaluation may combine quantitative performance measures with user feedback. The researcher therefore combined technical benchmarking, scenario-based decision-quality assessment and user-centred evaluation, and deferred agronomic trials to future work, a combination that the six targets of Table 3.4 make explicit. A further reflection is that most reviewed studies report evaluation as a separate, one-off activity; building the evaluation metrics into the artefact itself, so that each DSRM cycle can be assessed on demand, is a methodological refinement that this study contributes.

## 2.6 Reflection and Justification of the Research Gap

The technology areas reviewed above each demonstrate strong individual value, yet meaningful integration across them remains challenging in the existing literature and in commercially available systems. IoT systems can collect substantial volumes of environmental data, yet raw measurements do not, by themselves, translate into practical agricultural decisions; a gap remains between data acquisition and actionable insight unless a dedicated interpretation layer is present (Kour and Arora, 2020; Sinha and Dhanalakshmi, 2022). AI-based systems can support this interpretation, but their practical usefulness depends critically on the quality and completeness of the underlying data and on the appropriateness and transparency of the decision logic applied; poorly calibrated sensors or opaque rule sets undermine the credibility of AI-generated recommendations (Adadi and Berrada, 2018). E-commerce platforms can meaningfully improve market connectivity, yet they remain architecturally disconnected from the operational farm information that would allow listings and transactions to reflect current production reality (Zhang and Berghäll, 2021; Lezoche et al., 2020). Digital twins promise to integrate observation and prescription, but the twins actually reported are largely monitoring dashboards (Verdouw et al., 2021; Pylianidis, Osinga and Athanasiadis, 2021).

A second, related limitation concerns architectural fragmentation more broadly. Smart-agriculture solutions surveyed in this review are designed around a single core capability, sensing, analytics or marketplace functionality, with limited attention paid to the interfaces that would allow these capabilities to function as a coherent whole. A third limitation concerns the human context: few of the reviewed systems address language, digital literacy or trust, despite the evidence that these determine adoption among smallholders (Klerkx, Jakku and Labarthe, 2019).

The key research gap identified for this study is therefore the limited availability, in both the academic literature (all sources reviewed were published within the last ten years, and the majority within the last five) and commercially deployed systems, of integrated platforms that connect field-level IoT monitoring with explainable, verifiable AI decision support and export-oriented B2B commerce within a single, multilingual architecture. Existing approaches typically address one or two of these areas in depth, but the specific combination, and more importantly the deliberate architectural integration of all of them, remains comparatively unexplored. Figure 2.2 positions the gap at the intersection of the three core domains. The research reported in this thesis focuses precisely on this integration, positioning its contribution not merely as the sum of individually well-studied components, but as a coherent, evaluated artefact that demonstrates how they can interoperate in practice.

## 2.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter reviewed the main technological and conceptual areas underpinning the Smart Farming Web System: layered smart-agriculture architectures and digital twins, IoT sensor networks and communication protocols in precision farming, data validation, AI-agent-based and explainable decision support, natural-language interaction, agricultural e-commerce and B2B export workflows, interface design for multilingual and low-digital-literacy users, and security. Existing academic and commercial systems were compared against the capabilities required by the research, and none was found to provide all of them. The technological analysis justified the adoption of capacitive and digital sensing, a hybrid HTTP/MQTT ingest interface, deterministic validation, a rule-based explainable agent, REST with Server-Sent Events, a zero-dependency backend, explicit workflow state machines with human oversight, the seven-stage export process and the combination of DSRM with Scrum. The reflection framed fragmentation and the absence of explainability, verification and multilingual design as the research gap. Chapter 3 now translates these foundations into a concrete research methodology.
